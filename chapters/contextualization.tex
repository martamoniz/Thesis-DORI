\chapter*{Contextualization and Motivation}
Interventional radiology (IR) refers to image-guided therapeutic procedures
performed under real-time X-ray visualization \cite{6}. The number of these procedures has
increased over the past two decades worldwide. Interventional radiologists
and their teams are the most exposed group of medical radiation workers due to the prolonged
use of fluoroscopy and the proximity required during interventions \cite{10,55}.
Currently, protection strategies rely on lead garments
and structural shielding within the room \cite{37}. Compliance with established occupational dose limits is monitored
using passive dosimeters, which are worn either above or below the lead apron. These devices
are read \emph{a posteriori}, in the best-case scenario, once a month. As a result, there is no
immediate feedback during procedures and exposure incidents cannot be prevented \cite{46,67}.

Active personal dosimeters (APDs) offer a complementary solution by providing real-time auditory and/or visual feedback. The main benefit
of these devices in IR is not to prevent exceedance of annual dose limits, which have been extensively studied and are rarely
surpassed under standard conditions. Instead, APDs support
the principle of keeping exposure {\it as low as reasonably achievable }(ALARA) \cite{1,2,3}. These systems alert personnel to high-dose situations, promoting behavioural adjustments to reduce avoidable exposure. Adjustments may include reducing fluoroscopy time and frame rate, optimizing patient and operator positioning, and improve radiation shielding placement \cite{4}.

This dissertation explores the development of an APD system for IR, DORI
(\textit{DOs\'imetro para Radiologia de Interven\c{c}\~ao}), based on a plastic
scintillator coupled to a silicon photomultiplier. The primary goal is to design,
assemble and validate a pulse-mode front-end for this detector, in an architecture that can
be replicated to monitor several anatomical regions, namely the eyes, neck, thorax and
hands. Each unit is intended to be compact and fully wireless, so that it can be worn
unobtrusively. Taking advantage of the tissue equivalence of plastic scintillators~\cite{129}, a second
goal is to evaluate their use for personal dosimetry, through the implementation and validation
of methodologies for energy and dose calibration in the field of diagnostic radiology.

This document is structured in five chapters. Chapter 1 introduces the principles of IR and
the physics of radiation interaction and dosimetry. It also characterises IR as a high
occupational exposure environment and reviews APDs and their benefits. The next chapter introduces
the DORI concept and describes the design and validation of the front-end electronics.
The third chapter covers the methodology used for the energy and dose calibration of the detector,
along with the corresponding results and discussion. Chapter 4 shows the current DORI
prototype, resulting from successive design iterations, and reviews its wireless
communication and autonomy. In the last chapter, the global conclusions of the work are
presented and future work is discussed.
